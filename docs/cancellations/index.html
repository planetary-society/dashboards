About This Dashboard

This dashboard tracks NASA contracts and grants that have been cancelled, terminated, or significantly reduced since January 20, 2025. It is maintained by Casey Dreier at The Planetary Society to provide transparency into changes affecting NASA's programs and the organizations that support them.

The underlying record is a running ledger of every award the monitoring system has ever flagged. Awards are never deleted from it—they are reclassified as the federal record changes. The selector at the top of the Summary tab switches between four views of that ledger, and the Raw Data tab shows every row, including the ones no view displays.

What's an Award?

In federal spending, an award is money the government commits to a recipient. There are two main types:

  • Contracts — Agreements to purchase specific goods or services. NASA uses contracts to buy spacecraft, fund research and development, and hire technical support.
  • Grants — Financial assistance to support a public purpose. NASA awards grants to universities, nonprofits, and researchers for scientific studies, education programs, and technology development.

This dashboard tracks both types. The dollar amounts shown represent obligations—money the government committed to spend—which may differ from amounts actually paid out.

Why This Matters

NASA awards billions of dollars annually to universities, research institutions, and private companies across the United States. When contracts or grants are terminated, it can affect scientific research, student training, small businesses, and local economies. This dashboard helps citizens, journalists, and policymakers track these changes.

Data Sources

No single government source publishes a list of cancelled NASA awards, so the system watches several at once:

  • USAspending.gov — The authoritative federal spending database. All award details (recipient names, funding amounts, dates, locations) come from this source, and it is searched directly for termination transactions.
  • NASA Procurement Data View — NASA's contract modification database, monitored for termination and stop-work language in the text of contract modifications.
  • DOGE's published list — The savings claims posted by the Department of Government Efficiency for NASA contracts and grants.
  • NASA grants archive — NASA's own grants search, used to catch discontinued or shortened grants.
  • A local copy of the USAspending database — Queried directly for patterns the public search tools do not expose well: formal termination action codes (including on large ordering vehicles), performance periods pulled in sharply by a modification that also removes money, and grant clawbacks that take back a substantial share of an award.

How Awards Are Identified

Each of those sources is an independent detection net. An award enters the ledger when any one of them fires. The Sources column in the downloadable data records which nets flagged a given award, and the system also records the specific evidence—for example, a terminate-for-convenience action on a named modification and date, an end date pulled in by hundreds of days, or a clawback of a stated dollar amount and percentage.

The nets differ in how strong their evidence is. A formal termination action code is close to conclusive; an end date that suddenly moves up is a signal that something happened, not proof of a termination. That difference is why the Summary tab offers four views instead of one number:

  • Cancelled — Awards with affirmative evidence of termination: a formal termination action, termination or stop-work language in the award record, or a clawback. This is the default view and the basis for the headline count. Awards flagged only by a shortened end date are excluded here, as are descopes — awards where part of the work was cut but the award itself continues.
  • DOGE Claims — Every award DOGE has publicly claimed credit for, regardless of what the award record later showed. This view compares claimed savings against the money actually obligated and flags divergence between the two.
  • Suspicious — Awards whose only evidence is a truncated performance period: the end date moved up, but there is no termination action code, no termination language, no clawback, and no outside claim. Awards whose end date moved later on net are excluded from this view — a flagged date change that extended the award is not a cut. These are leads worth checking, not confirmed cancellations.
  • Reversed — Terminations that did not stick: rescinded, set aside by a court, or awards that kept receiving money after being flagged.

Every visitor sees one view at a time. The counts, dollar figures, map, and tables all reflect the view currently selected.

How Confident Are We in These Numbers?

Not every row rests on equally solid ground, and the dashboard says so rather than averaging the difference away. Each award carries an evidence tier—a plain answer to the question "how do we know?"—shown as a badge in the Raw Data table and on each award card, and summarized for the whole view in the evidence bar at the top of the Summary tab. The four tiers, strongest to weakest:

  • Federal record — A termination or deobligation action appears in USAspending or FPDS federal contracting records, found either by our daily source sweeps or by the weekly re-verification of the award's transaction history. This is the government's own accounting of what it did.
  • NASA list — The award appears on NASA internal cancellation or grant-termination lists, but no federal termination action has turned up yet. NASA is a credible source about its own awards; the paperwork simply has not caught up.
  • Inferred — Detected by comparing daily USAspending snapshots: the award's end date was quietly cut short. Nobody announced anything; this is our own inference from a change in the record.
  • Uncorroborated — The only evidence is DOGE's own claim. No federal record, no NASA list, and no weekly re-check corroborates it. A claim the record later confirms moves up to Federal record, so this tier is the residue that nothing else backs.

A view made mostly of federal records is a much firmer number than one made mostly of claims, and the evidence bar is there so that difference is visible before you quote a total.

Re-checked every week

Once a week, every award in the ledger has its full federal transaction history walked again from scratch, and the result is recorded with the date it was checked. That is how reversals get caught, how closed-out awards get relabeled, and how a termination that never materialized stops being counted as one. The date of the most recent sweep is printed beneath the evidence bar.

Sometimes the weekly check disagrees with the listing. When an award is carried as cancelled but no termination action appears anywhere in its federal transaction history, it is marked with a next to its status. That usually means the cancellation is real but was recorded only on NASA's internal lists; occasionally it means the award was never terminated at all. Either way, it is flagged rather than smoothed over.

What became of the DOGE claims

Claims get the same treatment, and the DOGE Claims view breaks them into four outcomes: a termination was verified in the award's federal transaction history; the award simply expired on schedule with no termination action at all; no termination was found in the federal record; or the claim was reversed or otherwise resolved—reinstated, vacated by a court, descoped, or excluded from our totals. The mix shifts as the record fills in, so the current breakdown is shown on the page rather than quoted here.

Reading the monthly timeline

The timeline on the Summary tab buckets each award by month. For DOGE claims, that month is when the claim first appeared. For every other view, it is the month of the latest federal contract action on the award—a proxy for when the termination landed, not a recorded event date. It is usually close and occasionally not, so treat the shape of the timeline as an approximation of timing rather than a precise record of it. Awards with no usable date are counted in the note beneath the chart instead of being plotted.

Claims vs. Outcomes

The dashboard keeps two different kinds of fact apart, because they can disagree.

A claim is someone's public assertion that an award was cancelled—who said it, what they said it saved, and when. Today the only claimant is DOGE. Claims are recorded once and never removed, whether or not the award turned out to be genuinely terminated. If a claim is later restated, the revision is appended alongside the original rather than replacing it.

An outcome is what the federal award record now shows: whether the total obligated amount has grown or shrunk since the award was first flagged, and whether its end date moved. Outcomes are recomputed from the live data every time the ledger is rebuilt.

Divergence is the comparison between the two. An award that was claimed as a saving but has since received more money is labeled accordingly, and it stays in the DOGE Claims view. This is a factual comparison of a public claim against the public record, not a judgement about intent.

When Terminations Are Reversed

Terminations are not always final, and the ledger records what happened next rather than quietly dropping the award:

  • Rescinded — A later modification cancels the termination and the award becomes active again. Six Princeton University grants terminated in April 2025 were rescinded by modifications posted in December 2025.
  • Vacated by court order — In September 2025, a federal court order set aside the termination of several Harvard University research grants. The modification text filed in the federal record states that "the termination has been vacated and set aside pursuant to the order entered on September 3, 2025, in President and Fellows of Harvard College v. US Department of Health and Human Services et al."
  • Continued — The award was flagged, but the record shows it kept receiving new obligations afterward, so no cancellation took effect.

Reversals are moved out of the Cancelled view once they have been confirmed in the award's transaction history. An award whose reversal has been detected by the automated weekly check but not yet confirmed by hand remains in the Cancelled view; the automated verdict is visible in the Auto Status column of the downloadable data. Several of the Harvard grants above are in that state today.

What Is Deliberately Excluded

Some flagged awards are removed from every view on purpose, because including them would overstate the effect of policy decisions. They remain visible in the Raw Data tab, where the Status column marks them Excluded and records the reason:

  • Termination for cause — The government ended the award because the recipient failed to perform. That is a contracting dispute, not a policy cancellation.
  • Actions before January 20, 2025 — Awards terminated before the tracking window opened. Detection sometimes surfaces them; they are outside the scope of this dashboard.
  • Descopes — Awards where a modification cut part of the work while the award itself continues. Calling a contract that is still active and still being funded "cancelled" would overstate the effect, so descoped awards appear only in the Raw Data tab, marked Descoped.

A small number of awards are also held back as needing manual review when the available evidence is not sufficient to classify them either way.

Important Notes

  • Updated daily, re-verified weekly — The sources are checked each day. Once a week, every flagged award is re-checked against its full USAspending transaction history to see whether the termination still stands, was closed out, or was reversed. The result of that check is published in the Auto Status and Auto Verified Date columns of the CSV download. A failed lookup is recorded as unresolved and retried; it is never treated as a finding.
  • Counts are not comparable over time — Detection was expanded in July 2026 to cover large ordering vehicles, end-date truncations, and grant clawbacks. The headline count rose substantially as a result. That reflects better detection, not a sudden wave of new terminations.
  • Not exhaustive — This list captures awards meeting specific detection criteria. Cancellations that leave no trace in these sources will not appear.
  • Further verification recommended — Inclusion reflects evidence in federal data. Before publishing, confirm details with the recipient or with NASA; corrections are welcome and are applied to the ledger.
