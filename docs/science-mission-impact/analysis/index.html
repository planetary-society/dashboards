Analysis

Assessing scientific productivity of NASA’s space missions by cost.

A. H. D. Koeppel and C. Dreier, The Planetary Society. Data as of Oct. 30, 2026.

Introduction

One of the stated goals of the current NASA administration is maximizing science per dollar.1 Another is faster time to science.2 For decades, NASA has struggled to reduce science mission costs and increase project cadence.3 Despite cost overruns and schedule challenges, the National Academies have consistently highlighted the unique value of NASA’s large strategic missions.4 However, the National Academies’ decadal survey5 and congressional statute6 also state that frequent, low-cost science missions are a cornerstone of a balanced program portfolio.

Programmatic experiments aimed at reducing cost and development time with low-cost missions are underway. NASA’s CubeSat, SIMPLEx, and Commercial Lunar Payload Services (CLPS) programs have produced dozens of low-cost missions across all science divisions since the late 2000s. Ride shares, commercial hardware, reusable components, and in-kind contributions have further driven down costs.7,8

Recent amendments to NASA’s competitive mission selection process have the stated aim of decreasing development and selection time.2 NASA originally aimed for two New Frontiers competitions per decade and Discovery mission launches every two years, but budget uncertainty has stalled announcements, producing a 10-year gap for New Frontiers (next selection expected 2027) and a gap of more than seven years for Discovery. Meanwhile, development of new directed science missions, such as the Aeolus and Skyfall missions to Mars and PROMISE Rover to the Moon, is proceeding quickly.

The recent uptick in the number of directed and low-cost missions, paired with the changes for competed missions, reflects a reprioritization. To prepare for precisely this type of restructuring, the National Academies’ 2017 report, Powering Science, recommended NASA develop a publicly accessible database that might be used to assess the value of different mission classes.4 Nearly a decade later, NASA has not released such a database.

To inform current discussions in the mission science community with quantitative evidence, we compiled a comprehensive database covering 141 science missions launched since 1980. We focused on science impact, cost, and time to impactful science. The overview gives the short version; this page sets out the analysis.

Measuring impact

Research community sizes and publication cultures differ across NASA’s five science divisions: Earth Science, Planetary Science, Heliophysics, Astrophysics, and Biological and Physical Sciences. Thus, we define science impact relative to the body of work retrieved for each division. We define “field-defining” publications as those in the top 1% of citation counts for their respective division. “High-impact” works are in the top 10%. “Above-average” works are in the top 50%. Each paper’s citations count over the four years from its publication; the Methods page explains how we gathered the papers, citations and costs.

Citation thresholds are a common approach to quantifying impact, used by NSF, universities, and others. We use them two ways.

Time to science

Results from past missions show that the relationship between cost and “time to science,” measured from start of formulation to the publication date of a mission’s first high-impact paper, is not linear. (Here each paper is ranked on all its citations to date, and failed missions are left out.) Very low-cost missions can take years to publish a highly cited result, largely negating their rapid development advantage: of the 26 missions of $50M or less that didn’t fail, only six have one, after a median 5.8 years. Very expensive missions return high-impact results quickly once operations begin (a median 0.2 years for those costing more than $1.55B, against 2.3 for missions of $50M or less; Figure 1), but face protracted development times (11.5 years from formulation).

Low-cost ($50M to $150M), but not CubeSat-class, missions have historically provided the minimum time to science, with a median of 5.5 years from formulation to first high-impact paper, though missions of $150M to $350M were close behind at 5.7. However, no single mission in our evaluation set returned a high-impact result in less than 2.7 years from formulation (QuikSCAT, a rapid reflight of the NASA Scatterometer mission, cut short in 1997 when the Japanese ADEOS satellite carrying it failed).

Science impact

The dataset indicates that a portfolio weighted more heavily toward missions costing $150M or less would reduce the volume of high-impact publications (Figure 2). The median mission costing $150M or less returned 0.18 N50 per $1M; for missions of $150M to $1B the median was 1.59; for missions of more than $1B the median was 1.81.

Of the 46 analyzed missions costing $150M or less, 14 (or 30%) have failed or fallen short of their goals due to a technical issue, while only 8.4% of all other missions did. This means that under historical risk postures, in order for low-cost missions to sustain the publication output of larger missions, the average successful low-cost mission would have to be about 32% more productive for the cost. In fact, the $150M-or-less group averages 3.2 high-impact papers per mission, compared with 115 for larger missions (in the four divisions we compare). Normalized by cost, they are less cost-efficient than larger missions at drawing citations in three of those four divisions.

Divisional cost-sensitivity

The data also reflect a strong sensitivity of low-cost mission output to division (Figure 4). Heliophysics missions have had more high-impact publication productivity in the $150M-or-less classes than any other division. Astrophysics missions costing $150M or less have produced less than one high-impact paper between them, but missions in the $150M to $350M range have produced the greatest return per dollar spent. Likewise, in Earth Science, missions costing more than $1.55B, and in Planetary Science, missions in the $750M to $1.55B range, have produced the greatest return per dollar spent.

These differences reflect the varying technical requirements of different science goals. For example, a $200M mission in low Earth orbit and a $200M mission landed on another planet do not belong to a common class that can predict scientific return. These distinctions indicate that a fixed cost cap does not carry the same meaning across all of NASA space science.

Success at low cost

Considering divisional differences, Astrophysics, Heliophysics, and Planetary Science have each produced successful missions at the low end of their division’s cost range with a high ratio of high-impact publications to mission cost: HaloSat in Astrophysics; ELFIN, TRACE, MinXSS-1, and FAST in Heliophysics; and Lunar Prospector in Planetary Science. Earth Science has none by this measure.

To gain insight into common themes that could inform program strategy for low-cost programs, we interviewed PIs and managers from four Heliophysics missions, three Astrophysics missions, two Earth Science missions, and one Planetary Science mission. Those conversations revealed three common keys to success:

  1. A focused guiding science objective driving development.
  2. High PI/manager authority with streamlined institutional oversight.
  3. Post-launch funding for scientific analysis and open data access.

Selection strategies

Most low-cost missions in our set (40 of the 45 with a recorded selection method), including nine of the 10 captured in interviews, were competed (Figure 6). Among mid-sized missions ($150M to $1B), where both types are well represented, competed missions have produced more impactful science output per dollar: a median 1.85 N50 per $1M, against 1.30 for directed missions.

CLPS missions are too immature to include in the main analysis, but as of Oct. 30, 2026, they have collectively produced fewer publications, in total and per dollar, than any other successful surface mission at the same point after the start of prime operations (Figure 7).

Maximizing science per dollar

Our results do not support investing in more directed and low-cost missions purely as a means of reducing the cost of science. Among the most efficient missions in terms of high-impact publications per dollar have been competed mid-to-large missions (e.g., Discovery, New Frontiers, MIDEX, Earth System Science Pathfinder). Given the outsized success of these missions, accepting more risk in the mid-to-large tiers may yield cost savings.

Beyond science

The cases for missions of different sizes extend beyond cost alone. Small missions broaden participation, train early-career researchers on flight hardware and testing, build institutional capacity, and mature affordable technologies that other missions and industry later adopt. Large strategic missions develop radically new technologies, access domains once thought impossible to investigate, sustain entire fields for decades, and offer high-profile opportunities for international visibility and collaboration. These factors should each be weighed alongside science when gauging the value of a new program.
