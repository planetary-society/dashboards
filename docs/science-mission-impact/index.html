Science Mission Impact: how does science impact and productivity scale with cost?

Skip to divisions

Exploring the scientific productivity of NASA's science missions

Scroll to begin

NASA policy is moving to smaller, higher-risk, commercial-supported science missions.

We decided to investigate: Can smaller missions produce high scientific productivity? Can they return science faster? Can they replace larger projects?

Blue Ghost Mission 4 (artist’s concept). Credit: Firefly Aerospace

To find out, we studied the output of 141 NASA-led science missions launched from 1980 to 2024, across all five of the agency’s science divisions.

What we found

  1. Replacing larger missions with more low-cost missions will not maintain scientific output.
  2. Small missions fail at higher rates than large ones, particularly in Planetary Science.
  3. Once science operations begin, larger missions produce high-impact science faster than smaller missions.
  4. The science output of NASA’s commercial lunar missions has not matched the productivity of science-focused missions.

CubeSats deployed from the International Space Station, Feb. 3, 2026. Credit: NASA

What we did

We assembled a set of 142,738 peer-reviewed papers associated with these 141 science missions published over their active lifetimes.

Papers published in peer-reviewed academic journals are a proxy for scientific productivity.

When one paper builds off the work of another, it cites the original work. Citations are one measure of scientific impact.

Together, the mission papers have been cited 6,131,659 times.

Science papers are not cited equally.

For example, half of all planetary science papers in our database accrued nine or fewer citations in their first four years.

The most cited paper received 260.

A paper is considered high-impact if it has more citations than 90% of its peers.

For example, in planetary science, papers with 31 or more citations within their first four years are high impact.

Midsize and larger mission classes contribute the overwhelming volume of high impact papers.

On this chart, the 37 planetary science missions are ordered by their cost, adjusted for inflation.

Each bar is a mission’s average yearly output of high impact papers, counting every paper from the start of its prime mission through 2023.

Cassini produced the most: about 13 top-10% papers a year.

Larger missions generate more high impact papers per year in every science division.

Most small missions in each division produced no top-10% papers at all.

Here, each astrophysics mission is placed by its cost and by the citations it drew for every $100M.

If small missions had greater productivity for their money, the trend line would slope downward.

If size made no difference, the line would be flat: overall citations per dollar would be the same.

The best-fit line through the 24 astrophysics missions with citations is positive. More expensive missions, on average, resulted in more citations per dollar.

The overall trendlines show a modestly positive relationship between cost and citations per dollar across all four major divisions.

Cheaper missions fail more often than more expensive ones.

Of the 46 costing $150M or less, 14 (about 30%) failed or fell short of their goals. More expensive missions failed eight times (about 8%).

Failure rates vary across NASA's science divisions.

Two out of 25 Astrophysics missions failed or fell short of their goals.

One out of five Biological & Physical Sciences missions failed or fell short of its goals.

Five out of 41 Earth Science missions failed or fell short of their goals.

Six out of 33 Heliophysics missions failed or fell short of their goals.

Eight out of 37 Planetary Science missions failed or fell short of their goals.

Below $350M, Planetary Science 7 out of 11 failed.

Time-to-science

Measured from the start of project formulation to the publication date of the first high-impact paper, missions of $50M to $150M returned high-impact science in a median duration of 5.5 years, with missions of $150M to $350M close behind at 5.7.

Once science operations begin, larger missions produce high-impact science faster than smaller missions.

Counted from the start of science operations, missions of more than $1.55B took a median of 0.2 years to a first top-10% paper, against 2.3 for missions of $50M or less.

NASA's Commercial Lunar Payload Services program purchases space on commercial lunar landers for science instruments.

The first mission launched in 2024, too recent to evaluate scientific impact through citations. So instead, we looked at scientific productivity.

In the 33 months since the first attempt, 16 peer-reviewed papers were published.

For comparison, Two previous NASA science missions to the Moon and Mars resulted in more publications by this same point.

So far, CLPS mission science productivity is falling short of prior science missions.

Small missions are still worth flying. They train scientists and engineers, prove new technology, and give more teams a way into space.

But larger missions drew more citations per dollar in three of the four divisions we compared, and small missions failed more often.

None of this says what a mission should cost. See how we measured it and each division’s record below.
