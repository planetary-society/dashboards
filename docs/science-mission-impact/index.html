Science Mission Impact: how does science impact and productivity scale with cost?

Skip to divisions

Can small missions replace NASA’s larger science missions?

A Planetary Society analysis of 141 NASA science missions. Data as of Oct. 26, 2026.

Scroll to begin

 

The White House’s 2027 budget request would cut NASA science by 46% and end 53 missions. Mars Sample Return, VERITAS, and the Geospace Dynamics Constellation have been canceled or delayed indefinitely.

NASA is shifting toward smaller, higher-risk spacecraft. We looked at whether they can make up for the science of larger missions.

Blue Ghost Mission 4 (artist’s concept). Credit: Firefly Aerospace

We studied 141 NASA-led science missions launched from 1980 to 2024, across all five of the agency’s science divisions.

We found 142,820 peer-reviewed papers associated with these missions over their active lifetimes.

A peer-reviewed paper is a proxy for scientific productivity.

Other scientists build on those papers. When one paper references another, it cites it.

Together, these papers have been cited 6,120,311 times. Citations are a measure of scientific impact.

Citations pile up unevenly. Take planetary science: half of its 10,928 papers drew nine or fewer citations in their first four years, and the most cited drew 260.

Those few, heavily cited papers are the ones other scientists build on most: the high-impact papers.

Counting only those first years keeps older papers from looking more impactful just for having had longer to collect citations.

We measure a mission’s science by its top-10% papers, the 10% most cited in its division. Fields cite at different rates, so each paper is ranked only within its own division.

In planetary science, the cutoff is about 30 citations.

Citations show how much other scientists used a result, not a mission’s full value. What citations miss

We call a mission small if it cost $150 million or less, adjusted for inflation to 2025 dollars. Of the 141 missions, 46 are small, and half of those cost less than $7 million.

The line is our choice, not NASA’s. Why $150 million

Small missions fail more often.

Of the 46 small missions, 14 (about 30%) failed or fell short of their goals. Of the 95 larger missions, eight (about 8%) did.

All 12 outright failures cost $58 million or less. We count a failed mission as producing no science.

Most small missions produced no top-10% paper.

Of the 29 small missions that worked, 11 had a top-10% paper, compared with 84 of the 87 larger ones.

Back to planetary science, now with each of its 37 missions placed by cost. The seven left of the line are small.

Cassini alone produced 195 top-10% papers. The seven small missions produced about 11 between them.

Stacked from cheapest to costliest, the bars add up to a running total of the division’s 1,093 top-10% papers.

The seven small missions make up 19% of its missions but account for 0.96% of those papers.

Small missions make up 19% to 48% of each division’s missions but produced less than 1% of the top-10% papers in Astrophysics, Earth Science, and Planetary Science.

In Heliophysics, they produced 8.3% of the top-10% papers, and TRACE alone produced 69% of that share.

Small missions cost far less, so the fairer test is per dollar. Counting every citation, larger missions drew more per dollar in Astrophysics, Earth Science, and Planetary Science, even with any one mission left out.

In Heliophysics, small missions lead only because of TRACE; without it, larger missions do.

Through CLPS, the Commercial Lunar Payload Services program, NASA buys rides for its instruments on commercial Moon landers instead of building its own.

The first launched in 2024, too recently for citations, so we counted papers. In 33 months, four landers have produced 16 peer-reviewed papers, at a combined cost of $621 million.

Two earlier NASA missions to the Moon and Mars each had more papers on their own than all four landers combined.

In their first 33 months, LCROSS ($139 million) had 24 and Mars Pathfinder ($613 million) had 67.

Blue Ghost M1 and IM-2 Athena began work more than a year after the first CLPS launch, so they’ve had less time to publish.

Small missions are still worth flying. They train scientists and engineers, prove new technology, and give more teams a way into space.

But they can’t make up for the science of larger missions. Larger missions drew more citations per dollar in three of the four divisions we compared, and small missions failed more often.

None of this says what a mission should cost. See how we measured it and each division’s record below.
