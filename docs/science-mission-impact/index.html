Science Mission Impact: can smaller, cheaper missions do the science of big ones?

Skip to divisions

The impact and productivity of NASA’s science missions

What can 141 NASA missions tell us about the relationship of cost to scientific output?

Scroll to begin

NASA has repeatedly experimented with smaller, cheaper, and riskier science missions.

But no broad survey of scientific performance had been done to validate these efforts.

We asked four questions:

  1. Is there a relationship between mission cost and scientific impact?
  2. Does lower cost mean more mission failures?
  3. Does time to science scale with cost?
  4. Are commercial lunar landers producing similar science to NASA-led missions?

To answer them, we studied the output of 141 NASA-led science missions launched from 1980 to 2024, across all five of the agency’s science divisions.

What we did

We assembled a set of 142,768 peer-reviewed papers associated with these science missions.

Papers published in peer-reviewed academic journals are a proxy for scientific productivity.

When one paper builds on another, it cites it. Citations are one measure of scientific impact.

Science papers are not cited equally.

For example, half of all planetary science papers in our database accrued nine or fewer citations in their first four years.

The most cited paper received 260.

We call a paper high-impact when it drew more citations in its first four years than 90% of the mission papers in its field.

In planetary science, that means 31 or more.

Some stand out: TRACE (Heliophysics) produced high-impact papers at a rate comparable to much more expensive missions.

Most produce few or no high-impact papers.

Dividing each mission’s citations by cost compares citations per $100M spent. This can be thought of as scientific impact per dollar.

If lower-cost missions consistently returned more impact per dollar, we would expect the average citations to be higher on the left side of the plot.

If average impact per dollar were similar across costs, the line would be approximately flat.

This is the actual running average of impact per dollar. In planetary science, very low-cost missions returned poor results, primarily due to mission failures.

How we calculate the averages

In no science division did very low-cost missions demonstrate better impact per dollar than larger ones.

Conversely, NASA’s most expensive science missions did not consistently demonstrate an advantage, either.

Shading shows a 95% interval for the average at each cost.

Mission failure rates

Very low-cost missions (the first two columns) have a full or partial failure rate of 30.4%.

For missions costing above $350M, that rate is 5.6%.

Planetary Science has one of the highest failure rates for very low-cost missions.

Below $350M, seven out of 11 Planetary Science missions between 1980 and 2024 failed.

The program’s most expensive failure, Mars Observer, succumbed to a hardware issue traced to the use of a commercial spacecraft bus designed for Earth orbit.

Time to high-impact science

Lower-cost missions commonly take about 5 1/2 years to produce a high-impact science paper, when measured from the start of project formulation to the paper’s publication date.

More expensive missions tend to take longer due to longer development and transit times before science operations can begin.

If you measure from the start of science operations, more expensive missions tend to lead to high-impact papers almost immediately.

Scientific productivity of CLPS missions

The four Commercial Lunar Payload Services (CLPS) missions flew too recently to compare scientific impact using citations.

Instead, we can compare their scientific productivity by counting the number of papers published since launch.

Compare CLPS output with two science-first NASA missions. These also had short mission durations and mixed experimental technology demonstration and science.

So far, CLPS landers are trailing in scientific productivity.

What we found

  • Very low-cost missions do not provide a clear advantage in science per dollar, and neither do very expensive ones. For most divisions, the mid-cost missions tend to return excellent efficiency.
  • High failure rates. Of missions below $150M, 30% failed. Only 8% of larger ones did. Failure rates vary across scientific divisions.
  • Not reliably faster. Most low-cost missions generate no high-impact science papers. Those that do often take as long as mid-size missions.
  • CLPS has yet to demonstrate significant scientific productivity. Each mission has, so far, produced fewer papers than comparable NASA-led science missions.

Very low-cost missions are worth doing: They help train future mission leaders, prove out new technology, and can return good science.

But nothing in this record demonstrates they can replace the scientific impact of more complex and costly missions.

Conversely, flagship missions are worth doing, too. Citation efficiency is not the only measure of value. Larger missions tend to break new ground, provide unique data, and develop technologies that enable subsequent smaller missions.
