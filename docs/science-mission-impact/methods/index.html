Methods

How we compare mission costs with tracked publications and citations.

Data as of Oct. 20, 2026.

Why it’s hard to count a mission’s science

Space is hard, as the saying goes. So is measuring a space mission’s scientific output.

No list of a mission’s science publications is perfect; some are just more complete than others. Large missions, like the Hubble Space Telescope, assign staff to identify science papers and separate results from engineering descriptions and speculative work. A team studying the European Space Agency’s science missions describes a move from manual review to machine learning and natural language processing, with scientists still checking the selections. Reviews like these can require the full text of many papers, often behind paywalls.

What counts as “science” varies, and it takes many judgment calls. We used a simpler approach, one used in many peer-reviewed studies such as Fiore and Elvis (2026): we searched NASA’s Astrophysics Data System (ADS), the leading database of space science papers and citations, for each mission’s name and the names of its main instruments. Publications stand in for scientific productivity, and citations to them stand in for impact.

We tailor each search to weed out obvious false matches from shared names and acronyms: TRACE, for example, is both a space mission and a very common word. We also checked our results against the hand-curated publication lists that several missions keep. They generally agree within 10% to 25%. The one exception is GALEX, which differs widely because many papers don’t name the mission in their abstracts. Since we’re comparing mission costs that differ by orders of magnitude, that is well within acceptable limits, and rerunning our comparisons with the curated lists gave the same overall results.

We’re looking for broad patterns, not a perfect count. That’s why we call these records “tracked publications”: they are the papers we found, and the list may have gaps.

What we included

The study covers 141 missions, launched from 1980 to 2024, that NASA’s science directorate primarily built and funded, across five of its divisions. It focuses on U.S.-led, free-flying missions whose publication records are complete enough to compare. By default, each paper collects citations over its publication year and the three calendar years after.

When a mission joins the study

A mission joins once its record has matured. Its prime mission has to be over, and two more years must pass for its results to reach print. Then the last of those papers need their own citation window: the year each appeared and the three calendar years after it. We admit a mission three months before that final year closes. A mission lost with no papers joins two years after it ended, since it has no citations to wait for.

That rule leaves out recent missions for now, including OSIRIS-REx, DART, the James Webb Space Telescope, Mars 2020, and Parker Solar Probe. Each will join as its record matures. Entry depends on the prime mission, not the Active Mission Window, so missions whose prime missions ended only recently, such as OSIRIS-REx, Mars 2020, and Parker Solar Probe, have to wait.

Comparisons stay within each division. Research communities differ in size, publishing practices, and citation rates, so a higher count in one field does not mean its science is more valuable.

Why Biological & Physical Sciences isn’t compared

All five of its missions cost $150 million or less, so there are no costlier missions to compare them with. They count in the study’s mission totals and failure figures, but not in the cost comparisons.

Small missions are newer

In the compared divisions, half the missions at $150 million or less launched in 2018 or later; half the costlier ones launched by 2003. Giving every paper the same citation window accounts for how long it has had to collect citations, but the two groups still come from different eras of NASA’s science program.

The $150 million line

We split missions at $150 million in 2025 dollars, counting a mission at exactly that cost as small. It is an editorial line, chosen to set apart the very small missions NASA is now leaning on. It is not a threshold found in the data, and nothing here says what a mission should cost.

Most of the groups we compare hold few missions. When a division has only a handful of small missions, one of them can move the group’s numbers, which is why each division page names the missions its figures rest on.

Failures and partial outcomes

We count a mission as having failed or fallen short when our records list it as a failure, partial failure, or partial success. A failure counts as zero output in every comparison; a partial failure or partial success counts whatever it published.

Across all divisions, 14 of the 46 missions at $150 million or less failed or fell short, 12 of them lost outright. Of the 95 costlier missions, eight did. Allowing for chance in groups this size (a 95% Wilson interval), the underlying rate plausibly lies between 19% and 45% for the small missions and between 4.3% and 16% for the costlier ones; the two ranges don’t overlap.

Within a single division the counts are too small to compare rates this way, so division pages give counts, not percentages.

What citations measure, and what they don’t

A citation shows that other scientists used a result. It doesn’t measure a mission’s value to the public, the technology it proved, or the people it trained. The Leiden Manifesto for research metrics makes the same point: numbers should support judgment, not replace it.

A top-10% paper is one of the 10% most cited of all its division’s papers in the selected window, every publication year ranked together. Fields cite at different rates, so each paper is ranked only against papers from its own division. In the two windowed views, every paper’s citations are counted over the same span of years, so an older paper has no head start; in the Lifetime view it does, and older papers fill more of the top 10%. Papers tied at the cutoff share the last places, so a division’s top 10% is exactly a tenth of its papers. Papers that describe a mission or its instruments count like any other; for a mission with few papers they can hold much of its citation count.

Tracked publications

Tracked publications are the peer-reviewed research papers we found for each mission. They are not necessarily its full publication record. The study includes 142,687 distinct tracked publications.

We use mission-specific searches in NASA’s Astrophysics Data System (ADS), now available through SciX. Searches look for mission and instrument names in titles, abstracts, and keywords, with topic restrictions to distinguish ambiguous acronyms. Mission bibliographies help identify omissions, and we review each addition and exclusion individually.

This approach misses papers that use a mission’s data without naming it in those fields, and a name match alone doesn’t show that a paper used the data. In effect, we measure how strongly a mission’s name shows up in paper titles, abstracts, and keywords.

What we exclude

We exclude non-refereed material, conference proceedings, news articles, and records for datasets or catalogs.

When tracking begins

Searches start with the first full month after prime science operations begin, or at launch if no start date is published. That cuts down on prelaunch and overview papers, though it doesn’t weed out every false match. For missions with long cruises or flyby results, we also include results published before the prime mission began.

Comparison windows

Older papers have had more time to collect citations. The two windows give every paper the same citation period; lifetime totals show everything to date.

Active Mission Window

The default comparison covers publications from a mission’s operating life and allows two years after mission end for results to appear. Each paper’s citations count from its publication year through the three calendar years that follow. A window counts as mature three months before its last citation year ends, so the most recent papers’ final citation year is counted only through the as-of date (at least nine of its 12 months), and their counts can still grow slightly. For a mission still operating, or one that ended too recently, the window stops at the latest date that has matured; a window cut to less than two years isn’t scored.

Prime Mission Window

This comparison follows the prime phase, with two years after prime mission end for results to appear. Its length varies by mission.

Lifetime

All tracked publications and their reported citations to date.

Mission costs

Costs come from NASA’s reported life-cycle costs at launch. We include documented partner contributions where available. These costs generally cover development, payloads, launch, and prime operations. Values are adjusted to 2025 dollars using NASA’s New Start Inflation Index.

Costs and citations cover different spans

Costs stop at the end of prime operations, while the default comparison counts papers from a mission’s whole operating life, extended missions included. That is deliberate: much of a mission’s longevity is paid for up front, in how it is designed and built. Extended-operations costs may be added in a later version.

Citations per dollar

To get citations per $100 million, we divide each group’s citations in the Active Mission Window by its total cost. We say one side leads only when it stays ahead with any single mission left out. Otherwise, we name the mission the result rests on, or say neither side clearly leads.

Corrections

If you find a missing or misattributed paper, a wrong date or cost, or any other error, write to casey.dreier@planetary.org. Mission pages list the query and every tracked publication, which makes a specific correction easy to check.
