Methods

How we compare mission costs with tracked publications and citations.

Data as of October 30, 2026.

The challenges of bibliometrics for space science missions

Space is hard, as the saying goes. So is measuring a space mission’s scientific output.

There is no perfect collection of science publications associated with a mission; there are only degrees of correctness. Large missions, like the Hubble Space Telescope, dedicate staff time to identifying science papers and distinguishing results from engineering descriptions and speculative work. A European team studying ESA’s space science missions describes a transition from manual review to machine learning and natural language processing, with scientists still validating the selections. Such assessments can require access to the full text of large numbers of publications, often shielded by paywalls.

Exactly how one defines “science” varies, and many judgment calls are required. For this study, we used a simpler approach also used in many peer-reviewed publications, such as Fiore & Elvis (2026): querying NASA’s Science Explorer (SciX) database by mission name and the names of its primary instruments. SciX is the leading repository of space science publication and citation references. This approach assesses the signal of a mission within the abstract literature, using publications as a proxy for scientific productivity and citations to those publications as a proxy for impact.

We tailor each query to filter out obvious false positives from name or acronym collisions: TRACE, for example, is both a space mission and a very common word. We also compared our query outcomes to the managed collections of several missions with hand-curated publication sets. They generally agree within 10%–25% of publication records, with a single exception, GALEX, differing by a wide amount due to the pattern of not reporting the mission name in many abstracts. Since we’re primarily comparing mission costs across orders of magnitude, this is well within acceptable limits. We tested our outcomes with curated mission collections and found the overall comparative outcomes remained consistent.

Our aim is to identify broad patterns in the research associated with these missions. We call these records “tracked publications” to make their scope clear: they capture the papers we found, while leaving room for omissions and corrections.

What we included

This dataset contains data related to 141 projects built or funded by NASA’s science directorate between 1980 to 2024, spanning all five divisions.

The default comparison allows papers the publication year and three following calendar years to accrue citations.

Comparisons stay within each division. Research communities differ in size, publishing practices, and citation rates, so a higher count in one field does not mean its science is more valuable.

Tracked publications

The publications we tracked are the peer-reviewed research papers associated with each mission using a set of keywords in their abstract, title, and keywords via NASA's Science Explorer digital library (SciX). Publications are time-gated by the start of mission operations and continue through the full mission's lifetime, plus two years to accomodate general publication times.

Our SciX queries use mission and major instrument names along with topic restrictions to distinguish ambiguous acronyms. The query for each mission is listed in the mission's overview page.

This approach will miss papers that use mission data without naming the source in those fields. A name match alone also does not establish that a paper used the data.

In essence, we are measuring the signal strength of a mission's name in the SciX abstract fields. That is a common approach to bibliometrics, though more complex approaches are possible (see De Marchi, G., & Parmar, A. N., 2024).

What we exclude

We have general exclusions for non-refereed material, conference proceedings, news articles, and records for datasets or data catalogs. We have mission-by-mission exclusions based on known false positives reviewed with AI and human oversight.

Citation comparison windows

Older papers have had more time to collect citations, which would artificially inflate their metrics compared to more recent ones. To account for this, all metrics in this dashboard (except for the explicit lifetime metrics) use windowed citation counts of three years, inclusive, from the publish date of each paper.

This means that missions launched after the full citation comparison window are not included in this study. A mission still operating limits publications to those have were published within this citation window.

Mission costs

Costs come from the NASA's reported life-cycle costs at launch. We include documented partner contributions where available. These generally cover development, payloads, launch, and prime operations. Values are adjusted to 2025 dollars using NASA’s New Start Inflation Index.

Running averages

The citations-per-dollar charts on the overview and division pages show a running average. Each mission’s rate is its windowed citations per $100M of its cost, and a mission that failed or published nothing counts as zero. At each cost, we average the rates of missions within a factor of 10 of that cost, weighting the closest missions most heavily. We only draw the average where at least three missions contribute.

The shaded band is a 95% range from resampling the missions. To compare small and larger missions, we average the rates on each side of $150M and only say one side did better when the 95% range of the difference excludes zero and the result holds with any single mission left out.

Citation-weighted output (N50)

Our analysis uses both threshold counts to determine high-impact papers and a citation-weighted count, defined as: Nc = papers + citations ÷ c, where c is a citation cutoff. We set c to each division’s top-50% cutoff in the Active Mission Window and call the result N50. Because each division has its own c, N50 normalizes outputs across different divisions.

How we used AI in this project

AI was used extensively in this project to help write and review code, to refine and improve our SciX queries, and to identify papers for exclusion based on our internal criteria. According to Planetary Society policy, AI was not used write our analysis: that was led by the authors. All AI-driven query suggestions and exclusions were reviewed by humans as well. Our public facing text is written by the authors using AI for copyediting. Some descriptive, functional text such as tool-tips on this dashboard are AI-generated.

Corrections
